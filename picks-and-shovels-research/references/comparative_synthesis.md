# Comparative Synthesis Playbook

Use this playbook when the user requests a comparative report, shortlist, ranking, peer analysis, fixed entry prices, catalysts, risks, or a strict no-chase framework. The output must synthesize evidence across companies rather than produce disconnected mini-reports.

## 1. Lock the comparison brief

Before research, record the reference date, universe, market-cap ceiling, exchanges, desired finalist count, budget, currency, fiscal-period basis, and whether the user wants one fixed entry price or three zones. Define the comparison unit: equity value versus enterprise value, reported versus adjusted metrics, trailing versus forward periods, and price as of the same date. If a value cannot be normalized, show it as unavailable rather than silently mixing bases.

## 2. Run parallel research batches

For each candidate, create the same evidence packet in parallel. The input list is one item per company, with these required fields: entity card; picks-and-shovels role; customer and workflow; direct competitors, substitutes, incumbent bundle risk, and internal-build alternative; moat causal chain; latest financial quality; balance sheet and dilution; valuation inputs; catalysts and dates; risks and thesis-invalidation signals; price history; and fixed-entry-price calculation. Do not rank candidates until every packet uses the same field structure.

A practical batch should use at least three times the desired finalist count. Assign identical research questions to each company, then perform a second normalization pass across the batch. Preserve an evidence ledger containing source URL, source type, publication date, fiscal period, currency, reported/derived label, and the exact claim supported.

## 3. Normalize before comparing

Use a comparison matrix with rows for companies and columns for product role, end markets, revenue, growth, gross margin, operating margin, operating cash flow, free cash flow, cash, debt, net cash/debt, share count, dilution, customer concentration, valuation multiples, moat evidence, catalyst quality, and risk severity. Mark every cell as reported, calculated, estimated, scenario, or judgment.

Select peers by business model and value-chain role first, then adjust for size, growth, margin, cyclicality, capital intensity, geography, balance sheet, and liquidity. Never apply a raw peer median when the company is materially different. Explain why each peer is included and preserve excluded peers with reasons.

## 4. Compare competitors and moats causally

For each finalist, identify the closest direct rival, functional substitute, large incumbent that could bundle the capability, and customer internal-build option. State the company’s specific win condition: performance, price, speed, availability, integration, qualification, service, or lifecycle economics.

Write the moat as:

> Capability → customer pain → qualification or switching friction → repeat purchase or embedded position → economic value.

Score each barrier separately. Use evidence for IP, process know-how, qualification, installed base, recurring consumables, data, manufacturing yield, service, distribution, and customer-specific integration. Do not call a theme, partnership, or patent count a moat without evidence of customer value or durable friction.

## 5. Assess financial quality

Use the latest comparable fiscal period and show the period end. Review revenue quality and growth, gross-margin direction, operating leverage, operating cash flow, free cash flow, working capital, capex, cash runway, debt maturities, covenant or refinancing risk, share-based compensation, equity issuance, warrants, convertibles, and customer concentration. Separate recurring revenue, backlog, orders, deployments, and announcements; an announcement is not recurring revenue.

Rate financial quality from 0 to 10 using the existing scorecard, but explain the score with evidence. A pre-revenue or heavily cash-burning company may remain in the universe only as a clearly labeled option-sized candidate and must not score like a proven compounder.

## 6. Value at the requested fixed entry price

A fixed entry price is an analytical threshold, not a guaranteed fair value or execution instruction. Derive it using the most appropriate method and show the math. Use DCF when cash flows are sufficiently observable; use EV/revenue or EV/EBITDA only when the peer set and margin path justify it; use cash-adjusted valuation for pre-profit companies only when the survival and dilution assumptions are explicit. For hybrid cases, triangulate at least two methods.

For a peer method, show: selected peers, inclusion rationale, current peer multiples, target multiple, company metric, enterprise value, net debt/cash, diluted shares, and implied price. For a DCF, show revenue growth, operating margin, tax, working capital, capex, discount rate, terminal growth or exit multiple, dilution, and sensitivity. Use conservative, base, and optimistic cases internally even when delivering one fixed price.

The fixed entry price should satisfy all three tests: the price is below the base-case analytical value by a stated margin of safety; the downside case does not imply unacceptable loss of capital or runway risk; and the thesis remains intact at the reference date. If any test fails, output “no fixed entry price—wait for evidence” rather than forcing a number.

## 7. Catalysts and confirmation gates

List catalysts in a table with event, expected timing, mechanism, leading indicator, confirmation evidence, upside relevance, and failure condition. Prefer catalysts that can be verified through filings, orders, backlog, customer qualification, deployment, gross-profit growth, recurring revenue, or reduced operating cost. Distinguish company-controlled execution catalysts from external macro or sentiment catalysts. Assign each catalyst a probability label only when the evidence supports it; otherwise call it a scenario.

## 8. Risks and thesis invalidation

Rank risks by probability, impact, time horizon, and detectability. Cover product or technical failure, qualification delays, customer concentration, pricing pressure, incumbent response, supply chain, regulatory or export controls, working capital, debt/refinancing, dilution, liquidity, accounting quality, management, and valuation compression. For each risk, state the monitoring metric and the precise fact that would invalidate the thesis.

## 9. Strict no-chase framework

Set a hard maximum purchase price before discussing catalysts. Do not raise it because the stock is moving, because a headline is exciting, because a peer re-rates, or because the user fears missing out. A catalyst never overrides a broken financial or valuation gate.

Use these rules:

| Condition | Action |
|---|---|
| Price is at or below the fixed entry price and the thesis gates pass | Eligible for the planned starter allocation; do not exceed the pre-set size. |
| Price is above the fixed entry price but below the hard ceiling | Wait for a pullback or fresh fundamental evidence; do not average the ceiling upward. |
| Price is above the hard ceiling | No chase. Put the name on a watchlist and record the next review trigger. |
| Price rises because of an unverified announcement | No chase until orders, qualification, deployment, revenue, or margin evidence appears. |
| Price falls while fundamentals deteriorate, dilution rises, or runway shortens | Do not catch the falling knife; reassess or abandon. |
| Thesis invalidation signal appears | Freeze new purchases and mark the thesis failed pending fresh work. |

Define the hard ceiling as the lower of the valuation-derived ceiling and the risk-adjusted budget ceiling. Recalculate only after a scheduled review or material new evidence, not intraday. Use limit-style language only as an analytical boundary; do not place trades.

## 10. Synthesis and ranking

Deliver the report in this order: executive conclusion; scope and method; entity cards; broad universe; elimination log; normalized comparison matrix; ranked shortlist; competitor and moat comparison; financial-quality comparison; valuation and fixed-entry-price table; catalyst calendar; risk and invalidation register; strict no-chase table; budget-aware sizing; diligence checklist; limitations; and references.

The final ranking must explain trade-offs. A higher score cannot hide a failed survival gate, extreme dilution, or a valuation that requires heroic assumptions. Include a direct view for each finalist: buy only at the stated threshold, watch, speculative option-size only, or exclude. State the strongest alternative and what evidence would change the ranking.

## 11. Quality checks

Before delivery, confirm that every candidate used the same fields; every material number has a source and period; peer multiples are comparable; fixed prices are reconstructable; catalysts have confirmation gates; risks have invalidation signals; hard ceilings were set before catalyst discussion; no-chase rules are explicit; and reported data, calculations, estimates, scenarios, and judgments are labeled separately.

For finance topics, include: “I’m an AI, not a licensed financial advisor—this is analysis, not guaranteed advice; investing carries risk you bear.”
