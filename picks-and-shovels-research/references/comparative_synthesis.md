# Comparative Synthesis Playbook

Use this playbook for comparative public-company research, ranked shortlists, peer analysis, fixed entry prices, catalysts, risk registers, or strict no-chase rules. The objective is not to produce several isolated company summaries. It is to create a normalized, evidence-weighted decision system in which every candidate is tested against the same questions and the ranking can be reconstructed.

## 1. Lock the brief and comparison unit

Record the reference date, universe, market-cap ceiling, exchanges, desired finalist count, account/budget context, currency, fiscal-period basis, price lookback, and requested output. Decide whether the report is discovery, finalist comparison, monitoring, or valuation. If the user asks for execution, stop at analysis and state that no trade will be placed.

Define the comparison unit before collecting numbers. State whether valuation is enterprise value or equity value; whether earnings are GAAP, IFRS, or adjusted; whether metrics are trailing, annualized, or forward; whether shares are basic or diluted; and whether values are nominal or inflation-adjusted. Record the as-of date for price, market cap, cash, debt, and share count. Never blend fiscal periods or currencies without labeling and reconciling them.

## 2. Build the parallel research architecture

When five or more independent candidates are being studied, run one standardized evidence packet per company in parallel. The input list is one candidate per item. Each packet must contain the same fields in the same order:

| Packet section | Required questions | Minimum evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Entity card | What is the legal entity, ticker, exchange, listing status, fiscal year end, currency, and reporting unit? | Regulator or official IR source |
| Role | What product or service enables which customer workflow and how many end markets? | Product documentation plus customer evidence |
| Commercial proof | Who pays, what is the revenue model, and what proves adoption? | Filing, order, backlog, deployment, or customer disclosure |
| Competitors | Who is the direct rival, substitute, incumbent, and internal-build alternative? | Company, competitor, and customer sources |
| Moat | What capability creates what friction and economic value? | Qualification, retention, pricing, yield, IP, or workflow evidence |
| Financial quality | What are growth, margins, cash conversion, working capital, capex, dilution, and runway? | Same-period statements and filings |
| Valuation | What do peer, DCF, and cash-adjusted methods imply? | Reconstructable calculations |
| Catalysts | What dated event can change evidence or estimates? | Filing, guidance, contract, deployment, or calendar |
| Risks | What can impair value, and what observation would invalidate the thesis? | Risk factor, historical, operating, or market evidence |
| Entry discipline | What is the fixed entry price, hard ceiling, and no-chase trigger? | Valuation and downside analysis |

Run the packets independently first to reduce anchoring. Then run a normalization pass across all packets. Do not rank a company because it has more narrative coverage than another. Missing evidence is a finding, not a zero-cost assumption.

Maintain an evidence ledger with: claim ID, company, claim, source URL, source type, source date, fiscal period, currency, reported/derived/estimated/scenario/judgment label, page or section, and whether a second source corroborates it. Maintain an elimination log with candidate, gate failed, evidence, and whether the name can return after a specific milestone.

## 3. Use a source hierarchy and evidence ladder

Use primary sources first: regulatory filings, official annual and quarterly reports, earnings releases, investor presentations, technical datasheets, patents, procurement records, and customer disclosures. Use independent technical, industry, academic, and reputable financial sources for triangulation. Use search results only to discover URLs, not as final evidence.

Rate evidence for each important claim:

| Level | Evidence | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Theme language, unverified claim, or promotional forecast | Do not use as proof |
| 1 | Product launch, partnership, patent, or management assertion | Signal only |
| 2 | Trial, qualification, order, backlog, deployment, or named customer | Commercial validation begins |
| 3 | Repeat revenue, installed base, recurring consumables/service, margin improvement, retention, or measured customer savings | Monetization and workflow embedment |
| 4 | Multi-period replication, independently corroborated demand, durable returns, or proven displacement | Strongest evidence of durability |

For every material conclusion, state the evidence level and what higher-level evidence is still missing. Do not treat a partnership as revenue, backlog as revenue, or a single customer as a diversified installed base.

## 4. Normalize the operating data

Create a financial data dictionary before comparing candidates. For each metric, record definition, period, units, currency, source, and formula. Use the same period where possible; if not, use the latest twelve months and disclose the mismatch. Convert currencies using a stated date and rate. Keep reported and adjusted figures in separate columns.

At minimum, calculate or report:

| Area | Diagnostics |
|---|---|
| Growth | Revenue growth, organic versus acquired growth, backlog/order growth, recurring-revenue share |
| Gross economics | Gross margin, contribution margin if disclosed, mix, pricing, utilization, and margin direction |
| Operating quality | Operating margin, R&D intensity, sales efficiency, fixed-cost absorption, and operating leverage |
| Cash conversion | CFO, CFO/revenue, CFO/net income, free cash flow, capex/revenue, working-capital drag |
| Balance sheet | Cash, restricted cash, debt, maturities, net debt/cash, covenants, revolver availability |
| Capital allocation | Buybacks, acquisitions, capex, R&D, dividends, insider purchases/sales, and dilution |
| Share economics | Basic shares, options, RSUs, warrants, convertibles, fully diluted shares, and dilution scenario |
| Concentration | Largest customers, end-market concentration, geographic concentration, supplier dependence |

Investigate earnings quality. Reconcile net income to operating cash flow; identify receivables or inventory growth that outpaces revenue; separate stock-based compensation from cash expenses while still recognizing its dilution; review capitalized costs, one-time adjustments, acquisition accounting, and non-GAAP reconciliation. A high growth rate with weak cash conversion is not automatically bad, but it requires a funding and working-capital explanation.

## 5. Test survivability and dilution

Estimate runway as cash and available liquidity divided by a clearly defined forward cash-burn measure. Use recent CFO plus capex, not only management’s adjusted burn. Show a base case and a stress case with slower revenue, lower gross margin, higher working capital, delayed customer payments, and a financing event.

Model dilution explicitly. Include in-the-money options, RSUs, warrants, convertibles, earnouts, ATM capacity, shelf registrations, and likely financing needs where disclosed. Use at least a current diluted-share case and a stressed dilution case. If a company requires frequent equity issuance to survive, lower the classification even if the technology is attractive.

## 6. Map competitors and substitutes

For every finalist, identify four alternatives: the closest direct rival, the functional substitute, the large incumbent that could bundle the capability, and the customer’s internal-build option. Compare them on performance, price, speed, availability, qualification, integration, service, supply assurance, and lifecycle economics.

Use a displacement test:

1. What customer problem is sufficiently painful to trigger a switch?
2. What proof is required before qualification or production use?
3. Who controls the buying decision and budget?
4. What would the incumbent do if the company gains traction?
5. Can the customer reproduce the capability internally at acceptable cost and risk?
6. What is the company’s specific path to win, and what evidence supports it?

Do not award a moat for being early, having a patent, using AI, or operating in a large market. Award moat points only where the capability creates persistent customer value and a barrier that is costly, slow, risky, or inconvenient to replicate.

## 7. Analyze the moat as a causal system

Write every moat as:

> Capability → customer pain → qualification or switching friction → repeat purchase or embedded position → economic value.

Test each possible source separately: process or materials know-how, patents and trade secrets, qualification and regulation, installed base, consumables, data, manufacturing yield, supply chain, service and repair, distribution, brand, network effects, and customer-specific integration.

For each source, record evidence of strength, evidence of erosion, time required for a rival to reproduce it, who captures the economics, and which metric should improve if the moat is real. Prefer multi-period evidence such as stable or rising gross margins, repeat orders, retention, pricing, return on invested capital, high utilization, lower customer failure rates, or declining unit cost.

## 8. Score quality without hiding failed gates

Use the default weighted score unless the user supplies another method:

| Category | Weight | Scoring question |
|---|---:|---|
| Strategic importance | 15% | Does the company enable multiple structural growth markets and solve a painful bottleneck? |
| Moat and defensibility | 25% | Is there demonstrated technical, qualification, workflow, manufacturing, or service friction? |
| Commercial proof | 15% | Are revenue, orders, deployment, retention, and customer economics verified? |
| Financial quality | 15% | Are growth, margin, cash conversion, capital intensity, and dilution attractive? |
| Valuation | 15% | Is the current price reasonable against conservative scenarios and comparable peers? |
| Survivability | 10% | Can the company fund its plan without unacceptable refinancing or dilution risk? |
| Alignment and governance | 5% | Are founder/insider incentives, governance, and capital allocation credible? |

Score each category from 0 to 10, show the rationale, and calculate `weighted score = Σ(score × weight)`. Apply hard gates before ranking: unverifiable public listing, no credible product, severe opaque financing risk, or thesis that depends on one application winning can exclude or cap a candidate regardless of its score.

Use A for evidence-backed asymmetric candidates, B for strong candidates with a material caveat, and C for interesting options whose economics or survivability do not yet support conviction. Never let a charismatic founder compensate for failed survival economics.

## 9. Build a deeper valuation bridge

Use at least two methods when the data permit and explain why each method is appropriate. The output is not “fair value”; it is a range of conditional analytical values used to set a fixed entry price.

### Comparable-company valuation

Select peers by value-chain role and economic model before size. Show inclusion and exclusion reasons. For each peer, collect enterprise value, revenue, gross margin, EBITDA or operating margin, growth, capital intensity, net cash/debt, and liquidity. Compare EV/revenue, EV/EBITDA, P/E, price/free cash flow, or other sector-specific multiples only when denominators are meaningful.

Adjust the target multiple for growth, margin, cyclicality, concentration, balance sheet, liquidity, and moat evidence. Show peer median, selected target multiple, company metric, implied enterprise value, net debt/cash, diluted shares, and implied price. Do not use a peer median without an adjustment explanation.

### DCF or cash-flow valuation

Show the bridge from revenue to free cash flow. State revenue growth, gross and operating margins, taxes, working capital, capex, depreciation, terminal growth or exit multiple, discount rate, net debt/cash, and diluted shares. Show forecast years and terminal-value share of enterprise value.

Use at least bear, base, and bull cases. Vary the two most consequential assumptions in a sensitivity table, normally discount rate versus terminal growth or exit multiple, and separately test growth versus margin. Explain what operating evidence would move the company from bear to base or base to bull.

A simple unlevered formulation is:

`UFCF = EBIT × (1 − tax rate) + D&A − capex − change in net working capital`

`Enterprise value = Σ UFCF_t / (1 + WACC)^t + terminal value / (1 + WACC)^n`

`Equity value = enterprise value − debt + cash − other senior claims`

`Implied price = equity value / fully diluted shares`

### Cash-adjusted and pre-revenue cases

For pre-profit companies, show cash, debt, quarterly burn, runway, milestone cost, and dilution scenarios. Use cash-adjusted EV only if the product and financing path are credible. If value is dominated by terminal assumptions or an unfinanced milestone, classify it as option-sized and avoid a false-precision price target.

## 10. Convert valuation into a fixed entry price

When the user asks for one fixed price, calculate it transparently. Start with a conservative base-case analytical value, then apply a stated margin of safety that reflects uncertainty, liquidity, dilution, cyclicality, and downside severity. The fixed entry price must be below the base case and must not depend on the bull case.

Use this decision sequence:

| Gate | Required test | Failure output |
|---|---|---|
| Valuation | Fixed price is below conservative base-case value by a stated margin of safety | No fixed entry price |
| Downside | Bear-case value, cash runway, and dilution do not imply unacceptable loss or financing risk | Watch or exclude |
| Evidence | Commercial and moat evidence support the base case | Wait for confirmation |
| Integrity | No thesis-invalidation signal is active | Freeze new work or abandon |
| Budget | Position size fits the user’s stated risk and liquidity constraints | Reduce size or do not participate |

Show the fixed price, basis, base-case value, bear-case value, margin of safety, share-count basis, and the exact evidence that would justify revising it. If the company is too uncertain to value, say “no fixed entry price—wait for evidence.”

## 11. Catalysts with probability and confirmation gates

Build a catalyst calendar with event, timing, mechanism, leading indicator, expected financial impact, evidence level, probability label, confirmation source, and failure condition. Separate company-controlled execution from external macro, industry, policy, or sentiment catalysts.

Use a staged catalyst test. First, identify the announcement. Second, identify proof such as order, qualification, deployment, backlog conversion, repeat revenue, gross-profit improvement, or measured customer savings. Third, identify the financial statement line or KPI that should change. Fourth, set the date by which the evidence must appear. A catalyst that never reaches the financial or operating KPI is not thesis confirmation.

Do not raise the fixed entry price merely because a catalyst occurs. Revalue only if the catalyst changes durable cash flows, risk, dilution, or terminal economics and the new evidence passes the same valuation gates.

## 12. Risk register and thesis kill criteria

Rank each risk by probability, impact, time horizon, detectability, and whether management can control it. Cover product failure, qualification delay, customer concentration, pricing pressure, incumbent response, supply chain, regulation/export controls, working capital, debt/refinancing, dilution, liquidity, accounting quality, management, governance, and valuation compression.

Use this risk table:

| Risk | Probability | Impact | Leading indicator | Mitigation | Kill criterion |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Example: customer concentration | Low/medium/high | Low/medium/high | Largest customer share or order timing | Diversification plan | Loss or non-renewal without replacement |

A kill criterion must be observable and falsifiable. Examples include a missed qualification milestone without credible recovery, gross-margin deterioration that shows the moat is not monetizing, runway falling below the milestone horizon, an unexpected financing that creates severe dilution, customer loss that invalidates the demand thesis, regulatory prohibition, or evidence that an incumbent can bundle the product at lower lifecycle cost.

## 13. Strict no-chase framework

Set the fixed entry price and hard ceiling before discussing catalysts or recent momentum. The hard ceiling is the lower of the valuation-derived ceiling and the risk-adjusted budget ceiling. Do not move it upward because the price is rising, a headline is exciting, a peer re-rates, or the user fears missing out.

| Price and evidence state | Decision |
|---|---|
| Price at or below fixed entry price; all gates pass | Eligible for planned starter allocation only; do not exceed pre-set size. |
| Price above entry but below hard ceiling; no new durable evidence | Wait; do not average the ceiling upward. |
| Price above hard ceiling | No chase; record a watchlist review trigger. |
| Price rises on announcement only | No chase until orders, qualification, deployment, revenue, or margin evidence appears. |
| Price falls while fundamentals remain intact | Recheck thesis, runway, dilution, and valuation before considering staged exposure. |
| Price falls with deteriorating economics or active kill criterion | Do not catch the falling knife; freeze or abandon. |
| New evidence changes durable cash flows | Rebuild the model from scratch; never simply ratchet the target price upward. |

Use a cooling-off rule after a sharp move: no revised entry price until a scheduled review, a new filing, or a confirmed operating milestone. This framework is analytical only and must never be phrased as an instruction to place an order.

## 14. Portfolio-aware sizing

For a small budget, use a core/satellite structure rather than five equal positions. A profitable, cash-generating company with a proven moat can be core; a proven but slower or less proprietary company can be secondary core; an early commercial candidate can be a satellite; and a pre-revenue or severe cash-burn name can be option-sized only. Do not invest money needed for essentials.

Size from downside and liquidity, not excitement. Show maximum loss under the bear case, dilution-adjusted exposure, position concentration, and what evidence would permit a second tranche. If fractional shares are unavailable, do not manufacture a diversified-looking portfolio whose position sizes are economically meaningless.

## 15. Required final report structure

Use this order: executive conclusion; scope, reference date, and comparison unit; methodology and source hierarchy; entity cards; broad universe; elimination log; evidence-quality summary; normalized comparison matrix; competitor and substitute map; moat comparison; financial-quality bridge; balance-sheet and dilution stress test; valuation methods and sensitivity tables; fixed-entry-price table; catalyst calendar; risk and thesis-invalidation register; strict no-chase framework; budget-aware sizing; diligence checklist; limitations; and References.

The conclusion must state the strongest candidate, the strongest alternative, the main uncertainty, the fixed price or “no fixed entry price” decision, the hard ceiling, and what evidence would change the ranking. Distinguish reported figures, calculations, estimates, scenarios, and judgments. Cite material factual claims with numbered Markdown references and include a References section.

## 16. Quality-control checklist

Before delivery, verify that every candidate used the same packet; every named company has a legal identity, ticker, exchange, fiscal year, and currency; the initial universe is at least three times the finalist count; every elimination has a reason; every material figure has a source and period; peer multiples are comparable; DCF assumptions and sensitivities are reconstructable; diluted shares and financing needs are explicit; catalysts have confirmation gates; risks have kill criteria; the hard ceiling was set before catalyst discussion; and no-chase rules are unambiguous.

Check that no announcement is treated as revenue, no peer multiple is used without normalization, no low share price is called cheap, no technical support is called intrinsic value, and no pre-revenue company is scored like a proven compounder. If evidence is insufficient, lower confidence or exclude the candidate rather than filling gaps with optimistic assumptions.
