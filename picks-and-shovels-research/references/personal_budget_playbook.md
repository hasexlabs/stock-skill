# Personal-budget playbook

Use this reference when the user gives a small amount of money or asks how to distribute a limited portfolio. Begin by separating **available risk capital** from money needed for food, housing, transportation, debt payments, emergencies, or near-term obligations. If the money is needed for essentials, recommend keeping it liquid rather than placing it in volatile small-cap equities.

## Core principle

A small account should not be forced into artificial diversification. Five tiny positions can create complexity without reducing the real risk. Prefer one or two understandable positions, or use fractional shares if the broker supports them. Never assume fractional shares, zero commissions, or the absence of spreads and taxes.

## Risk-role framework

| Company profile | Portfolio role | Typical share of a small speculative account | Rule |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Profitable, cash-generating, proven moat | Core | 40–60% | Largest position; still subject to company-specific risk. |
| Profitable but slower growth or less proprietary | Secondary core | 15–30% | Use to reduce dependence on one high-upside thesis. |
| High-upside with early commercial proof | Satellite | 10–25% | Size below core names and wait for milestones. |
| Pre-revenue or severe cash burn | Option-sized speculation | 0–10% | Assume a total loss and dilution; never average down automatically. |
| Unverified or promotional lead | Watchlist | 0% | Research further before committing capital. |

These are risk-control heuristics, not suitability rules. Adjust downward when the user has a short time horizon, limited emergency savings, debt, or low tolerance for loss.

## Example for a $31 risk budget

If the user insists on a five-name basket and fractional shares are available, a conservative small-cap mix could place roughly 40% in the strongest proven cash-generating business, 30% in the highest-upside profitable or near-profitable enabler, 20% in a steadier secondary name, 7% in a higher-volatility infrastructure name, and 3% in a pre-revenue option. The exact percentages must follow the current evidence and need not be used if one or two names are clearly superior.

If fractional shares are unavailable, choose one or two names rather than buying an arbitrary number of low-priced shares. Share count is not diversification; ownership value and business risk matter.

## Asymmetric-risk explanation

A stock purchased at $0.70 can lose approximately $0.70 per share if it reaches zero, but the investor’s total loss equals the total dollars invested. Explain the difference between per-share loss and portfolio loss. Also discuss dilution, reverse splits, bankruptcy, trading halts, liquidity, and the possibility that a successful technology never becomes a successful business.

## Staged-entry framework

Use staged purchases when the user asks for an entry price. A simple structure is a starter purchase near the first reasonable support or valuation zone, a larger purchase near the preferred zone, and a final tranche only after confirming that a deeper decline is not caused by thesis-breaking news. Do not encourage automatic averaging down.

## Monitoring rules

Define the next evidence that would justify adding, holding, or stopping. Examples include backlog conversion, customer qualification, product launch, revenue growth, gross-margin trend, cash runway, dilution, debt, customer concentration, and competitive displacement. A price falling below a target is not automatically a reason to buy; first determine whether the business has deteriorated.

## Communication requirement

Use direct language: “This is a speculative position, not a core holding.” State that the user can lose the full amount invested and that the analysis is not personalized financial advice. Never describe a position as risk-free or guaranteed.
