# Picks-and-Shovels Research

## Mission

Find public companies that sell the **tools, components, materials, instruments, infrastructure, or specialized services** required by several future industries. Prefer companies whose products solve a difficult physical or technical problem and can become embedded in customer workflows, rather than application-layer stories that depend on one winner.

The default personal-use profile is: small public companies, normally below a $2 billion market cap; real products or credible commercialization; founder or insider involvement where verifiable; exposure to physical AI, aerospace/defense, advanced manufacturing, semiconductors, power, water, sensors, or scientific infrastructure; and enough financial evidence to distinguish an investable compounder from a venture-style option.

This skill performs research and analysis only. It must never place trades, imply a guarantee, or confuse a low share price with a low valuation.

## User-specific defaults

Apply these defaults unless the user overrides them:

| Preference | Default rule |
|---|---|
| Universe | Publicly traded small/micro-cap infrastructure and enabling-technology companies |
| Market-cap ceiling | $2B, with larger companies retained only as clearly labeled benchmark or incumbent comparisons |
| Preferred end markets | Physical AI, aerospace/defense, robotics, semiconductors, advanced materials, grid/power, water, sensors, scientific tools |
| Quality preference | Real revenue, high or improving gross margin, positive operating cash flow when possible, manageable debt, and limited dilution |
| Alignment preference | Founder, technical founder, long-serving owner-manager, or meaningful insider ownership/control; verify rather than assume |
| Portfolio context | If the user gives a small budget, do not force five equal positions; use a core/satellite structure and keep pre-revenue names option-sized |
| User-facing output | Plain English, ranked tables, a direct view, one fixed target price if requested, major risks, and explicit thesis-invalidation signals |

## Decision tree

1. **Is this a discovery task or a finalist task?** For discovery, build a broad universe first. For a named company, begin with an entity card and primary-source verification.
2. **Is the company truly a picks-and-shovels supplier?** Map its product to the customer workflow and state what it enables. If the thesis depends primarily on one application winning, label it application-layer or downgrade it.
3. **Does it pass the survival gate?** Check listing, market cap, revenue/commercial evidence, cash runway, debt, dilution, and customer concentration. A pre-revenue company can remain only as a clearly labeled speculative option.
4. **Is there a moat?** Identify the barrier: IP, qualification, switching cost, installed base, manufacturing, materials know-how, data, service, distribution, or relationship. If none is evidenced, do not call it a moat.
5. **Can a large incumbent reproduce or bundle it?** Compare with the closest incumbent, substitute, and platform owner. A large competitor’s existence does not automatically invalidate the thesis; explain the specific displacement path.
6. **Is the newer method real?** Separate product announcements from proof such as orders, backlog, deployments, recurring revenue, customer qualification, gross-profit growth, or reduced operating cost.
7. **What is the correct role?** Classify as A, B, or C: A = asymmetric candidate with current evidence; B = strong company or opportunity with a material caveat; C = interesting option where scientific or strategic upside is not yet matched by investment economics.
8. **If a price is requested, is the thesis intact?** Use historical support together with current fundamentals. Give starter, preferred, and deep-risk zones; if asked for one fixed price, give one target but clearly label it analytical and conditional.

## End-to-end workflow

### 1. Parse and lock the brief

Extract industry scope, market-cap ceiling, exchange/listing requirement, founder/insider requirement, financial gates, final shortlist size, price-history lookback, reference date, budget, and whether the user wants research, a model, or an execution. Treat ambiguous examples such as “NVIDIA-type,” “Redwire-type,” “QUBT-type,” or “Element” as thematic discovery leads, not as factual classifications.

Write an entity card for every named company before analysis. The card must include legal/common name, ticker, exchange, listing status, fiscal year end, currency, reporting units, industry, and primary-source URLs.

### 2. Build the universe in parallel batches

Search by value-chain role. Use at least the following batches when relevant:

1. Space, aerospace, defense, naval, and satellite infrastructure.
2. Physical-AI sensors, robotics components, machine vision, navigation, and control.
3. Semiconductor equipment, testing, substrates, photonics, RF, and optical communications.
4. Critical minerals, advanced materials, battery materials, recycling, and manufacturing equipment.
5. Power electronics, grid control, nuclear/fusion supply chain, thermal management, and energy storage.
6. Data-center infrastructure, cooling, power quality, and specialized connectivity.
7. Scientific instruments, quantum hardware, biomarker tools, proteomics, and cell analysis.
8. Water, wastewater, ocean technology, underwater sensing, and industrial efficiency.

Start with at least three times as many candidates as the desired final list. Record ticker, exchange, product, value-chain role, approximate market cap, founder/insider lead, source URL, and initial concern.

### 3. Apply the screen and preserve eliminations

Eliminate or flag companies that exceed the size limit, are not verifiably public, have no credible product or customer evidence, rely almost entirely on a single application outcome, have severe cash-burn or dilution risk, or cannot be verified with reliable sources. Preserve the eliminated names and state the principal reason. Do not silently discard inconvenient candidates.

Use the following gates:

| Gate | Pass condition | Treatment if failed |
|---|---|---|
| Public listing | Verifiable exchange, ticker, and regulatory reporting | Exclude |
| Size | Normally below stated market-cap ceiling | Exclude or benchmark only |
| Product reality | Product, prototype, contract, or credible development evidence | Exclude if purely promotional |
| Picks-and-shovels role | Enables multiple customers, platforms, or end markets | Downgrade if single-application |
| Commercial evidence | Revenue, orders, backlog, deployment, or qualified customer | Label pre-commercial if absent |
| Survivability | Cash runway and financing needs are understandable | C or exclude if opaque/severe |
| Alignment | Founder/insider evidence is documented | Do not award points for unverified claims |

### 4. Verify finalists with primary sources

Use SEC or equivalent filings, official investor-relations pages, earnings releases, technical datasheets, patents, government procurement records, customer disclosures, partner announcements, and independent technical sources. Search results are for URL discovery only. Read the source page before relying on it.

For each finalist answer: Who pays? What physical or technical problem is solved? Where is the product in the value chain? What is reported revenue and growth? What are gross and operating margins? What are cash, debt, dilution, working capital, and cash runway? Who manages the company? Is founder or insider alignment verified? What are the closest competitors and substitutes? What evidence supports the moat? What would invalidate the thesis?

### 5. Analyze moat and competitors

Write the moat as a causal chain, not a slogan:

> **Capability → customer pain → qualification or switching friction → repeat purchase or embedded position → economic value.**

Score or describe each relevant barrier separately: patents and trade secrets; materials/process know-how; qualification and regulatory approval; installed base and consumables; data and software; manufacturing yield and supply chain; service and repair; distribution; and customer-specific integration.

For competitors, identify: the closest direct rival; the functional substitute; the larger incumbent that could bundle the feature; and the customer’s option to build internally. State whether the company wins through performance, price, speed, availability, integration, or lifecycle economics.

### 6. Determine whether the company follows newer methods

Use a four-level evidence ladder:

| Level | Evidence | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Theme language only | No demonstrated innovation |
| 1 | Product or partnership announcement | Early signal; not proof of scale |
| 2 | Trial, qualification, order, backlog, or deployment | Commercial validation beginning |
| 3 | Repeat revenue, installed base, recurring consumables/service, margin, or operating-cost proof | Method is being monetized |

Look for compact/integrated architectures, AI-ready sensors, edge processing, automation, digital configuration, recurring consumables or service, lifecycle support, partner-led commercialization, regional manufacturing, and adjacent-market expansion. Never treat an announcement as recurring revenue until filings or customer evidence support it.

### 7. Score and classify finalists

Use the default weights below unless the user provides different weights:

| Category | Weight | What to measure |
|---|---:|---|
| Strategic importance | 20% | Enables multiple structural growth markets and solves a painful bottleneck |
| Moat / defensibility | 25% | Technical, qualification, installed-base, switching, manufacturing, or workflow barrier |
| Founder / insider quality | 15% | Verified founder involvement, technical leadership, ownership, and long-term behavior |
| Economics / capital efficiency | 15% | Gross margin, operating leverage, cash conversion, capex, working capital, and dilution |
| Valuation | 15% | EV/sales, P/E, FCF yield, cash-adjusted value, and expectations embedded in price |
| Survivability / balance sheet | 10% | Cash runway, debt, liquidity, customer concentration, and financing risk |

Score every category from 0 to 10. Weighted score = sum of `(category score × category weight)`. Show the rationale and evidence for each score. Do not let a charismatic founder compensate for weak survival economics.

Use these classifications:

- **A:** Highest-conviction asymmetric candidate; current business evidence supports the strategic thesis.
- **B:** Strong company or attractive opportunity with a material caveat such as valuation, concentration, cyclicality, or moderate execution risk.
- **C:** Interesting option whose scientific or strategic upside is not yet matched by revenue, cash economics, or survivability.

### 8. Analyze price history when requested

Use the bundled `scripts/price_history.py` utility for real daily data when appropriate. Record the source, lookback dates, observation count, latest close, two-year low/high, median, 25th/75th percentiles, 20/60/120-day averages, maximum drawdown, and recent local lows/highs. Save raw daily data and the summary.

Use technical levels as context, not as intrinsic value. A support zone is stronger when it overlaps with reasonable cash-adjusted valuation, improving fundamentals, or a defined catalyst. A falling price is not automatically a bargain; it may be signalling deteriorating economics.

### 9. Produce entry prices and position sizing

Provide three zones unless the user requests only one:

| Zone | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Starter | Small initial exposure near support or reasonable valuation |
| Preferred | Best risk/reward zone if the business thesis remains intact |
| Deep-risk | Attractive only after confirming the decline is not fundamental deterioration |

If the user asks for one fixed price, state one target per ticker and identify the basis: technical support, cash-adjusted EV, earnings multiple, or a combination. Do not call it a fair value or guaranteed price.

For a small budget, use the budget framework below:

| Company type | Suggested role |
|---|---|
| Profitable, cash-generating, proven moat | Core; largest allocation |
| Profitable but slower-growth or less proprietary | Secondary core |
| High-upside but early commercial proof | Satellite |
| Pre-revenue or severe cash burn | Option-sized speculation only |

Do not invest money needed for essentials. If fractional shares are unavailable, do not manufacture a five-name portfolio with a very small budget. Do not execute a trade or ask for credentials.

### 10. Comparative synthesis with parallel processing

When the user requests a comparative report, ranking, peer analysis, fixed entry prices, catalysts, risks, or a strict no-chase framework, read `references/comparative_synthesis.md` and use its standardized evidence-packet workflow. Run one identical research packet per company in parallel when there are five or more independent candidates, then normalize fiscal periods, currencies, share-count bases, peer definitions, and reported versus derived metrics before ranking. Preserve an evidence ledger and an elimination log.

The comparative report must cover competitors and substitutes, moat evidence, financial quality, valuation at the requested fixed entry prices, catalysts with confirmation gates, risks with thesis-invalidation signals, and a strict no-chase framework. A fixed entry price is a conditional analytical threshold, never a guarantee or trade instruction. Set a hard ceiling before discussing catalysts, and output “no fixed entry price—wait for evidence” when valuation, downside, or thesis-integrity gates fail.

### 11. Deliver the report

Use this order: executive conclusion; scope and methodology; entity cards; broad universe; elimination log; ranked shortlist; score table; detailed company analyses; competitor/moat comparison; newer-method assessment; financial and valuation table; price/entry table; budget-aware sizing; diligence checklist; limitations; and References.

Cite factual claims inline with numbered Markdown reference links. Include a References section with source title and URL. Label reported numbers, calculations, estimates, scenarios, and judgments separately.

## Quality-control checklist

Before delivery, confirm that:

- Every named company has a legal identity, ticker, exchange, fiscal year, and currency.
- The initial universe is materially larger than the final shortlist.
- Every important exclusion has a reason.
- Every material factual claim has a primary or high-quality source.
- Reported financial data are not mixed across fiscal periods.
- Market cap and EV formulas are shown or reconstructable.
- Founder status is verified or explicitly marked unverified.
- Competitors, substitutes, and incumbent displacement paths are named.
- New methods are separated from marketing language and rated by evidence level.
- Pre-revenue and cash-burning companies are not scored like proven compounders.
- Entry prices are conditional targets, not guarantees or execution instructions.
- The final answer is clear about uncertainty and thesis invalidation.
- Comparative reports use standardized parallel evidence packets, normalized comparison units, valuation sensitivities, catalyst confirmation gates, risk kill criteria, and a pre-committed no-chase ceiling.

## Finance communication standard

For finance topics, include: “I’m an AI, not a licensed financial advisor—this is analysis, not guaranteed advice; investing carries risk you bear.” Give a direct view after stating the key uncertainty. Do not use hype such as “guaranteed,” “can’t lose,” or “the next NVIDIA.”
